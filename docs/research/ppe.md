# ppe (safety glasses and respirators for woodworking and sanding)
## safety glasses
- Look for ANSI/ISEA Z87.1 marking on the frame and lens; Z87+ means a high-impact rating, tested with a heavier projectile. Ordinary fashion glasses and sunglasses do not meet this.
- Polycarbonate lenses are the usual impact-resistant material; they scratch more easily than glass, so a scratch-resistant coating matters.
- Side shields or wraparound lenses block debris from the sides; flat front lenses leave gaps.
- Anti-fog coatings help with sweat and respirator breath; they wear off over time and with cleaning, and none is permanent.
- Goggles with indirect vents seal around the eyes and keep out fine dust, which suits sanding and routing; open-vent glasses let fine dust in.
- Over-the-glasses designs fit over prescription frames but are bulkier and do not seal against fine dust.
- Fit matters: nose pads, adjustable temples and a snug seal keep glasses from sliding when you look down at a workpiece.
- Clear lenses suit indoor shop work; tinted lenses help outdoors but not in a dim shop.
- Replace glasses when the lens is deeply scratched, cracked or the frame is loose; protection falls after hard impacts even if damage is not visible.
- Clean with a microfiber cloth and lens spray or water, not a dry shirt that grinds dust into the lens.
- Wear eye protection every time you run a saw, router, lathe, sander or nail gun; one flying chip is enough to cause serious injury.
- For turning and heavy chip work, a full face shield over safety glasses adds protection.
- Multi-pack glasses suit shared shops and spares; keep a spare pair near each tool.
## respirators
- A dust mask is not a respirator unless NIOSH-approved; look for the NIOSH marking and a filter class like N95 or P100 printed on the product.
- N95 filters at least 95 percent of 0.3 micron non-oil particles; P100 filters at least 99.97 percent; N, R and P refer to oil resistance.
- Fine wood dust and drywall dust are hazards; hardwood dust is considered a carcinogen in some settings, so use at least an N95 and prefer P100 for power sanding.
- Disposable N95s suit short light jobs; reusable half-mask respirators with replaceable cartridges seal better and are cheaper over time.
- Fit is everything: facial hair under the seal, a loose strap or the wrong size leaks; do a seal check each time by covering the filters and inhaling or exhaling.
- Organic vapor (OV) cartridges handle finish and solvent vapors; particulate filters do not stop vapors. Combination cartridges handle both.
- European markings: P1, P2, P3 and FFP1, FFP2, FFP3 are EN classes; P2 and FFP2 are roughly comparable to N95 but are not NIOSH approved, so check which standard applies where you live.
- Exhalation valves reduce heat and moisture; valved masks are comfortable but do not protect others from exhaled breath.
- Replace particulate filters when breathing gets harder or when they are damaged; store respirators sealed in a bag away from dust.
- Full-face respirators protect eyes as well but fog and add weight; they suit heavy vapor and dust exposure.
- Sanding lead paint, old finishes or asbestos materials is hazardous; follow lead-safe rules and do not sand asbestos-containing materials.
- Wear eye protection with a respirator; check that glasses seal cleanly without lifting the mask edge.
- Dust collection, a sander with a vacuum port and an ambient air filter reduce how much dust you breathe.
